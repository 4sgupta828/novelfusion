[00:00:22] Host: Welcome back to the show. Today I'm with Maya Lindqvist, co-founder and CEO of Kestrel, the fraud and risk platform. Maya, you spent nine years at a payments processor before founding Kestrel. What was the moment?
[00:01:10] Maya Lindqvist: There was a literal meeting. I was running risk operations, and we were celebrating a quarter where fraud losses hit an all-time low. And a founder of one of our merchant customers called me — furious — because we'd declined his own card buying from his own store. He said, "your system thinks my best customers are criminals." I pulled the data and he was right. Our best quarter for fraud was our worst quarter for his revenue. Nobody in the room had thought to look.
[00:03:05] Maya Lindqvist: The insight wasn't that the models were bad. The insight was that the entire commercial structure was upside down. Every vendor in the space priced on blocked volume or flat fees. Nobody made more money when the merchant approved more good orders. So I decided the company would charge on approved volume — we only win when good orders get through. Half the industry told me that pricing was insane. It's our single biggest close-rate driver.
[00:05:20] Host: You've said hiring risk analysts is broken. Unpack that.
[00:06:01] Maya Lindqvist: The industry hires risk people for pattern-matching and pays them to be suspicious. We hire for calibration — the ability to say "I'm seventy percent sure" and be right seventy percent of the time. In interviews we run a forecasting exercise with real anonymized cases. The candidates who ace it are almost never the ones with the longest fraud résumés. Suspicion is cheap; calibration is rare.
[00:07:44] Maya Lindqvist: And we made a rule that every risk scientist spends one day a quarter listening to merchant support calls. Not dashboards — calls. You cannot hear a small business owner describe losing a four-hundred-dollar order over a mismatched zip code and stay in love with your precision metric. The empathy is load-bearing. It changes what people choose to work on.
[00:09:15] Host: Biggest technical bet you got wrong?
[00:09:58] Maya Lindqvist: Graph features, first version. We spent eight months building a beautiful entity graph — shared devices, shared addresses, payment instrument reuse — and the lift was real but the latency was fatal. We were adding sixty milliseconds to checkout. In payments, sixty milliseconds is not an engineering detail, it is a product decision, and we had made it by accident. We rebuilt the whole thing as precomputed features with a five-millisecond budget. The lesson I repeat to every new engineer: at checkout, latency is a feature with a hard budget, and every model has to pay rent inside it.
[00:12:10] Host: What do you think the industry gets most wrong right now?
[00:12:51] Maya Lindqvist: Explainability theater. Vendors ship a "reasons" panel that lists the top model features, and everyone pretends that's an explanation. A merchant doesn't want to know that "feature 47 contributed 0.3" — they want to know what would have made this order approve. We rebuilt our explanations as counterfactuals: this order would have approved if the shipping address matched the card's billing region, or if the account were more than six days old. That is an explanation a support agent can read to a customer, and a customer can act on.
[00:14:33] Maya Lindqvist: I'll give you the strong version of the claim: in regulated risk decisioning, the explanation is the product. The score is a commodity — there are a dozen vendors within a point of each other on raw catch rate. Whether a human can understand, contest, and correct the decision is where the next decade of this market gets decided. Regulators are converging on the same view, which is convenient for us and inconvenient for the theater.
[00:16:40] Host: Where does agentic commerce fit into your roadmap?
[00:17:12] Maya Lindqvist: It's the biggest re-platforming since mobile. Today we can tell a human from a bot with behavioral signals. Within two years, the paying customer often will be a bot — a legitimate one, holding a delegated mandate from a person. The fraud question stops being "is this a human" and becomes "does this agent hold a valid mandate for this transaction, within this spend scope." We're building mandate verification now, and honestly, the standards work — who issues the mandate, who attests to it — matters more than any model we'll train. I'd rather lose a feature race and win the standards conversation.
[00:19:30] Host: Advice for founders selling into risk-averse enterprise buyers?
[00:20:05] Maya Lindqvist: Sell the rollback, not the rocket ship. Enterprise risk buyers have all been burned by a vendor migration that went sideways. The single slide that closes deals for us isn't the accuracy chart, it's the one showing the shadow-mode period, the segment-by-segment cutover, and the one-click revert to their incumbent stack. Courage is not their job. Making the change survivable for them is yours.
